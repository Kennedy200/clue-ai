Storytime videos are one of the easiest formats to start with, because the main ingredient is something you may already have: a good story. A narrator, a looping background and clear captions are enough. No camera, no studio, no on-screen presence required.

This guide walks through the whole process, from choosing a story to publishing, and points out where beginners usually lose viewers.

## What counts as a storytime video

A storytime short is a narrated story, usually between thirty seconds and about two minutes. The story might be a personal experience, a story submitted by a follower, a fictional piece you wrote, or a retelling of something in the public domain. The visuals are secondary. A calm looping background, a few images, or simple animated text carry the video while the narration does the real work.

Two things to settle before you begin:

- **Whose story is it?** If the story belongs to someone else, get their permission and credit them if they want credit. Do not lift stories from forums or posts and present them as your own. Copyright and community rules apply to text just as they do to video.
- **Is it true?** If you present a story as real, it should be. If it is fiction, say so in the description. Viewers lose trust quickly when invented stories are passed off as fact.

## Choose a story with a built-in shape

The best short stories have a clear shape even in sixty seconds:

1. **A situation** that is easy to understand immediately.
2. **A complication** that raises the stakes.
3. **A turn** where something changes or is revealed.
4. **A landing** that feels like an ending, whether funny, surprising or emotional.

If you cannot summarise your story in one sentence that includes a conflict, it probably needs trimming. Long, meandering stories work as multi-part series, but a single short needs focus.

## Write for the ear, not the eye

A script that reads well on a page can sound stiff when spoken. Read your draft aloud and fix anything you stumble over.

- Use short sentences. A listener cannot re-read.
- Prefer concrete details over general statements. "My landlord changed the locks while I was at work" is stronger than "I had a housing problem".
- Cut adverbs and filler. "Really," "very" and "basically" rarely help.
- Use names sparingly. Too many names make audio stories hard to follow.

Aim for roughly 130 to 160 spoken words per minute at a natural pace. A sixty-second video is therefore about 130 to 160 words. Pace varies by voice and speed settings, so check the real duration when you generate the audio.

## Open in the middle of the action

Storytime hooks work best when they start at the moment of tension. Instead of "So this happened a few years ago when I was in university," try "The exam hall door locked behind me, and my phone was still in my bag." Then, if needed, fill in background in a sentence or two. See our guide on short-form hooks for more patterns.

## Pick a voice that fits the story

A warm, steady voice suits personal stories. A brisker voice suits comedy. Whatever you choose, keep it consistent across a series so viewers recognise it. Preview a few voices on the same paragraph before you commit, and check that names and unusual words are pronounced correctly. If a name sounds wrong, try respelling it phonetically in the script, then restore the correct spelling in the captions.

## Choose visuals that support, not compete

For a storytime, visuals should be easy to ignore. Popular choices:

- **A looping background** such as soft gradients, particles or abstract motion. These never distract and are safe to use because you can generate them yourself.
- **Your own footage** such as a satisfying loop you filmed or gameplay you recorded, if you own it.
- **Still images with slow motion** that illustrate key moments. Use images you have the right to use, and keep credits for anything that requires attribution.

Avoid third-party clips you do not have permission to use. A copyright claim can remove a video or affect a channel.

## Make the captions do real work

Many viewers will watch with the sound off, and even with sound on, captions help people follow along. For storytime:

- Show one short phrase at a time, large enough to read on a small phone.
- Highlight the word being spoken, which helps the eye track.
- Keep captions inside the safe area so platform buttons do not cover them.
- Use strong contrast between text and background.

Our caption best-practices guide goes through sizes and colours in more detail.

## Control the pace

Pace is what separates a story people finish from one they abandon. Some guidelines:

- Vary sentence length so the rhythm does not drone.
- Leave a small pause before the turn in the story. Silence signals that something important is coming.
- If you are making a series, end each part on an unresolved moment and say clearly that there is a part two, then post it promptly.

## Build a repeatable process

When you make storytime videos regularly, a routine saves time:

1. Keep a notes file of story ideas and permissions.
2. Draft the script and read it aloud.
3. Generate the voiceover and listen once for mispronunciations.
4. Pick a background and caption style from your saved brand kit.
5. Render, watch the whole video once on a phone, and fix any caption that wraps awkwardly.
6. Write a clear title and description. Mention if the story is fictional.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Starting with a long introduction.** Get to the tension fast.
- **Overly busy backgrounds.** If viewers watch the background, they are not following the story.
- **Inconsistent audio levels.** Check loudness against other videos on the platform.
- **No ending.** A story that just stops feels unfinished. Give it a landing.
- **Claiming fiction is fact.** It damages trust when people find out.

Storytime rewards consistency more than perfection. Pick a format you can repeat, publish regularly, and pay attention to where viewers drop off. That information will improve your next script more than any template.

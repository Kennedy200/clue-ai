Every short-form video begins with the same problem: a thumb hovering over the screen, ready to swipe. You have about three seconds to give that thumb a reason to wait. Everything else in your video, the editing, the captions, the voice, only matters if the viewer stays past the opening line. That opening is the hook.

This guide is about writing hooks deliberately instead of hoping the first sentence turns out well. There are no magic formulas here and no promises about views. There are patterns that give viewers a clear reason to keep watching, and a way to test which ones work for your audience.

## What a hook actually has to do

A hook has three jobs, and it has to do them quickly.

First, it has to **signal relevance**. The viewer should think "this is about something I care about" within a second or two. A hook about saving money lands on someone who wants to save money. A hook that starts with a vague greeting lands on nobody.

Second, it has to **open a loop**. A loop is a question or a tension the viewer wants resolved. "I lost my entire savings in one afternoon" opens a loop. "Hi guys, today I want to talk about savings" does not.

Third, it has to **promise a payoff that is believable**. If the hook promises a secret that the video never delivers, viewers feel tricked, and they remember it. The best hooks make a promise the rest of the video keeps.

## Six hook patterns to start with

**1. The result first.** Show or state the outcome, then explain how. "This three-ingredient meal costs under a thousand naira, and it feeds four." The viewer sees the end and wants the path.

**2. The mistake.** People are drawn to avoiding errors. "The biggest mistake new freelancers make with their first invoice." Make the mistake specific, not generic.

**3. The question you can actually answer.** "Why does your phone battery die faster in the afternoon?" It works only if the video answers the question clearly and soon.

**4. The contrarian claim.** "You do not need a camera to start a channel." A claim that cuts against common advice creates friction. It must be defensible, because viewers will check.

**5. The story in the middle.** Skip the setup and begin at the most tense moment. "The door was already open when I got home." Storytime and recap videos live on this pattern.

**6. The direct promise with a number.** "Three edits that make any caption easier to read." Numbers set expectations about length and let viewers decide quickly.

## Write the hook last, then rewrite it

Most people write the script from the top, so the hook is the first thing they produce, when they know the least about what the video really says. A better order is to write the body, find the single most interesting thing in it, and move that to the front.

Ask yourself one question of every draft: **what is the most surprising or useful sentence in this script?** If it is buried in the middle, it belongs in the first line.

## Cut the throat-clearing

Openings that waste the three seconds are easy to spot:

- Greetings and channel introductions ("Hey everyone, welcome back").
- Announcing the topic instead of starting it ("In this video I will talk about").
- Apologies and hedges ("I know this is a bit long, but").
- Long logo animations or title cards.

Delete them. If you want a greeting, put it at the end, where it costs nothing.

## Match the hook to what you show

A strong spoken hook can be undone by a weak first frame. The text on screen, the image, and the first words should agree. If the voice says "this mistake cost me everything," the first frame should show something that supports that, such as bold caption text with the key phrase, rather than a blank background.

Captions matter here more than most people expect, because many viewers watch with the sound off. Put the hook in large captions from the very first frame. Our guide to caption best practices covers sizing and contrast.

## Keep the first line short

Spoken hooks work best when they can be said in one breath. A rough target is under about twelve words. Short lines are easier to caption, easier to understand over background noise, and leave room for a beat of silence before the next line.

## Test hooks one variable at a time

You will not know which hook works until you try several. A sensible approach:

1. Pick one video idea.
2. Write three different hooks for it using different patterns.
3. Make or render each version, and post them at different times or on different days.
4. Compare how many viewers stay past the first few seconds, using the retention view in your platform analytics.

Change only the hook between versions. If you change the topic, the length and the thumbnail at once, you will not learn anything.

Be careful with small samples. A video with a few hundred views can swing wildly for reasons that have nothing to do with the hook. Look for patterns across several videos rather than one lucky result.

## Do not promise what you cannot deliver

Clickbait that the video does not support might raise the first few seconds of retention and then collapse it. Platforms notice when viewers leave early, and so do viewers who feel misled. The strongest long-term strategy is a hook that is honest about the video, only sharper.

A simple check: after you write the hook, read your last line aloud. Does it pay off what the first line promised? If not, change one of them.

## A quick hook checklist

Before you render, run through this list:

- Does the first sentence make the topic clear to the right viewer?
- Does it open a question or tension?
- Is it short enough to say in one breath?
- Is the key phrase on screen in large captions from frame one?
- Does the end of the video deliver what the start promised?

Captions are not decoration. For a large share of viewers they are the main way to follow a short video: people watch in noisy places, in quiet places where they cannot play sound, or with hearing loss. Captions also help viewers who speak the language as a second language, and they hold attention because the eye has something to follow.

Here is how to make captions that are easy to read, accurate and consistent.

## Start with accuracy

Automatic transcription is a great starting point and rarely perfect. Before you style anything, read the full transcript and fix:

- **Names and brands.** Transcription often guesses at unusual names, places and slang.
- **Numbers and units.** Check amounts, dates and percentages carefully.
- **Homophones.** "There" and "their", "to" and "too".
- **Punctuation.** It affects how phrases are broken across lines.

If your content uses Nigerian English, Pidgin, or mixes languages, expect more corrections. A quick pass is worth the time: errors in captions are very noticeable.

## Size and placement

Most short-form videos are watched on a phone held at arm's length, so captions need to be larger than you think.

- Use a bold, plain, sans-serif font. Decorative fonts are harder to read at speed.
- Choose a size that lets a short phrase fit on one or two lines without shrinking. Test by viewing on an actual phone, not just on a monitor.
- Place captions in the lower middle area of the frame, away from the very bottom, where platform buttons, descriptions and progress bars sit. Also avoid the far right edge, where action buttons appear.
- Keep text within a consistent safe margin on all sides.

Each platform adjusts its interface from time to time, so check a test upload on the platforms you use.

## Contrast and legibility

Text that cannot be read against the background fails, however good it looks in a design tool.

- Use light text with a dark outline or shadow, or dark text on a light box. Both survive busy backgrounds.
- Highlight colours should contrast with both the base text colour and the background. A bright accent for the active word works well, as long as it is still readable on your backgrounds.
- Avoid placing text over a bright, high-detail area without a backing shape or outline.
- Aim for strong contrast. For text, a ratio of at least 4.5:1 against the surface behind it is a widely used accessibility target.

## Timing and chunking

How you split the speech into pieces matters as much as the style.

- Show a few words at a time, often three to six, rather than whole sentences.
- Break at natural phrase boundaries, not in the middle of a phrase. "I went to the" followed by "market yesterday" is harder to read than "I went to the market" followed by "yesterday".
- Keep each chunk on screen long enough to read. Very short flashes frustrate viewers, and long holds make captions feel behind the speech.
- Sync to the voice. Word-level highlighting, where the current word lights up, helps people keep their place.

Because Clue AI measures voice timing from the audio itself and takes word timestamps from the speech, captions stay aligned even if you change the voice or speed and regenerate.

## Word highlighting: use it with care

Word-by-word highlighting is popular because it guides attention. Used well it makes a video feel lively. Used badly it becomes noise.

- Keep the highlight change simple: a colour shift or a slight scale is enough.
- Do not animate every word with a bounce or a spin. Motion competes with the picture.
- Use one highlight colour across a video, so viewers learn to read it without thinking.
- Respect users who prefer less motion. Have a plain style available.

## Consistency across a series

Pick a caption style and keep it for the whole channel. Consistency builds a recognisable look and saves decisions every time. Store your choice in a brand kit: font, size, colours, outline, position and highlight colour.

You can still change style deliberately. A dramatic story might use a heavier look than a light tips video. Make that choice on purpose.

## Style options to try

- **Bold outline.** White text with a black outline. Reliable on almost any background.
- **Box highlight.** Text on a solid or translucent bar. Excellent legibility.
- **Karaoke.** The spoken word changes colour as it is said.
- **Minimal.** Small, clean text for calm content. Check that it is still readable on a phone.
- **Single word.** One large word at a time for punchy delivery. Best for short, high-energy lines.

Try two or three on the same clip, watch each on your phone, and choose the one you can read comfortably without effort.

## Accessibility beyond style

Accessible captions go further than looks.

- Consider including important non-speech sounds in brackets where they matter to meaning.
- Provide a downloadable caption file such as SRT or VTT where the platform supports it. Uploading a separate caption file lets viewers choose captions and may help with translation.
- Describe key on-screen text in your voiceover if it matters, for viewers who cannot see it.

## Translating captions

Translating captions can reach new audiences. Machine translation is a draft, not a final product. Have a fluent speaker check important videos, especially for idioms and tone. If you publish translated versions, label the language clearly.

## A pre-publish checklist

- Is the transcript corrected, including names and numbers?
- Are captions large enough to read comfortably on a phone?
- Do they sit in the safe area, away from platform buttons?
- Is the contrast strong over the whole video, not just the first frame?
- Do the chunks break at natural phrase boundaries?
- Is the highlight style simple and consistent?
- Have you watched the whole video once on a real phone with the sound off?

That last step catches most problems. If you can follow the video with the sound off, your captions are doing their job.
